Briefing for the Mayor's Office A small district municipality · Lima, Peru October 2026

AI agents for the district: what they are, what they cost, and how to start safely

Your plan is to use AI agents to automate municipal workflows. This briefing explains, in plain terms, what an agent actually does, which companies sell them, what the recommended option — xAI's Grok — charges for storage, execution and orchestration, what Peruvian law requires of a government that handles citizens' data, and a sensible way to collaborate with a provider: start with one small pilot, prove it, then expand.

The short version

1

An agent does work, not just talk. It reads the district's own documents, answers residents, fills forms, routes files and drafts replies — staff approve anything official.

2

Three serious suppliers exist. xAI (Grok), OpenAI and Anthropic all sell agent platforms today. The government discounts you may have seen in the news are U.S.-only — Peru buys at commercial rates.

3

At district scale, the AI itself is cheap. On xAI's published prices, a citizen-attention agent costs roughly $100–150 a month in usage. The real cost is the one-time setup and the local team that runs it.

4

Peruvian law allows this — with homework. Cloud AI abroad is not banned, but the district must name a data-protection officer and an AI officer, keep a human in charge, and register the system. Details in section 5.

01

What an AI agent is — and is not

A chatbot answers questions. An agent completes tasks: it looks things up, uses software tools, and carries a workflow from the first message to a finished result, asking a person for approval at the points that matter.

1 · A resident asks

"What do I need to renew my business licence?" — on WhatsApp, the website, or by phone, in Spanish, at any hour.

→
2 · The agent checks the district's own documents

It searches the ordinances, fee schedules (TUPA) and procedures the municipality itself uploaded — and answers from those, not from the open internet.

→
3 · It acts

It books the appointment, generates the form, checks the file's status, or drafts the reply letter for the responsible office.

→
4 · A person approves

Anything official — a resolution, a fine, a payment commitment — is signed off by a municipal officer. The agent prepares; the human decides.

A simple chatbot

Answers from a fixed script. When the question doesn't match the script, it fails or invents. It cannot check a file, book anything, or learn from your documents. Useful for a short FAQ page — nothing more.

An agent (what this briefing covers)

Reasons over your real documents, follows multi-step procedures, connects to the district's systems, speaks and writes in Spanish, and hands difficult or sensitive cases to staff with a summary already prepared.

02

What it can automate in a district municipality

Six workflows where districts lose the most staff hours today. All six run on the same foundation: the district's documents, uploaded once and kept up to date.

Resident attention, 24/7

Requirements, fees, deadlines and office hours for every trámite — licences, permits, certificates — answered instantly on WhatsApp and the website, nights and weekends included. This is the natural first pilot.

Mesa de partes — document intake

Incoming letters and requests are read, classified, summarised and routed to the right office automatically, with a tracking number for the resident. Staff stop re-typing and start deciding.

Instant answers from district documents

Staff ask in plain language — "what does the ordinance say about noise after 10 pm?" — and get the exact passage, cited. The same document store that powers the resident-facing agent serves the whole municipality.

Drafting and summaries

Meeting minutes, file summaries, draft responses and reports produced in minutes instead of hours. Every draft goes to a named officer for review and signature — the agent never issues an official document on its own.

A voice agent for the phone line

Many residents — especially older ones — will never use an app. A voice agent answers the district's phone in natural Spanish, handles the routine questions, and transfers the rest to a person. xAI prices this at a flat $0.08 per minute.

Reports for the mayor's office

A nightly or weekly digest: what residents asked about most, which offices have files waiting, complaints by topic and neighbourhood. The agent turns its own activity logs into management information.

The boundary that keeps this safe: agents inform, prepare and route. Decisions with legal effect — approvals, sanctions, disbursements — stay with municipal officers, exactly as Peruvian AI regulation requires (section 5).

Can the district actually buy and run these in Lima?

Yes — with one verification step. OpenAI and Anthropic both list Peru as a supported country today. xAI does not publish a country list at all, so the Grok route begins with a simple live test: open the account, add billing, and run the agent — a day of work that settles the question before any contract.

On infrastructure: no major cloud provider runs a full region in Peru. The nearest are São Paulo and Santiago, and AWS operates a Local Zone in Lima, so the district's own application and document layer can sit in-country while the AI model runs abroad — a split Peruvian law expressly contemplates (next sections). The national government also runs its own state cloud, the Plataforma Nacional de Gobierno Digital (Nube Perú), for public entities. And Lima has local implementation firms that build exactly this kind of agent — among them Pulzar, CENESA, Emetix and Astratech — so the district does not have to hire foreign consultants to get started.

There are early local proofs that this works in Peruvian government: the Ministry of Transport runs "LIA", a 24-hour AI assistant answering citizens' questions nationally, and Miraflores already operates AI video-surveillance for citizen security. No Lima district has yet published a generative-AI citizen agent — which is precisely the opportunity: a well-run pilot would be a first.

05

Government and data privacy: the Peruvian rules

A municipality holds sensitive data — names, addresses, tax and property records. The good news: Peruvian law does not prohibit cloud AI or processing abroad. It requires the district to run it properly. Two legal frameworks apply, and both were modernised in the last two years.

Personal data protection

LEY N° 29733 · NEW REGULATION DS N° 016-2024-JUS, IN FORCE SINCE 30 MARCH 2025
  • A Data Protection Officer is mandatory for public entities, registered with the national authority (ANPDP).
  • Security incidents must be reported to the ANPDP within 48 hours — and to affected residents where their rights are at stake. Fines for failures reach 50 UIT.
  • Residents must be told when their data feeds automated decisions or profiling — transparency is a legal duty, not a courtesy.
  • Sending data abroad is allowed when the destination offers adequate protection or the contract guarantees it (standard clauses / data-processing agreement). It is a paperwork exercise, not a ban.
  • The district must keep a documented security policy and data inventory, and be able to prove compliance on demand ("proactive accountability").

Artificial intelligence in the public sector

LEY N° 31814 · REGULATION DS N° 115-2025-PCM · NATIONAL AI STRATEGY (ENIA) 2026–2030
  • Every public entity must name an AI Officer (OIA) and adopt an AI Action Plan of at least three years under the national strategy now in force.
  • Human oversight is mandatory, above all for high-risk uses: a person must be able to review, override and answer for the agent's outputs.
  • AI systems and datasets used by the State must be registered in the Catálogo IA Perú, the national catalogue run by the PCM's digital government secretariat (SGTD).
  • Privacy and security must be built in by design, and Peru's framework expressly provides for controlled test environments (sandboxes) — the legal basis for running a small pilot first.
  • There is no rule forcing government data to stay inside Peru; the State itself uses foreign cloud services alongside its own Nube Perú platform.

Five working rules for the district

  • Contract on business terms, never consumer accounts. All three vendors commit, on their API/enterprise terms, not to train their models on your data, to delete it within 30 days, and (xAI, OpenAI and Anthropic alike) to offer zero-retention arrangements. Consumer chatbots carry none of these protections — Anthropic's consumer plans even train by default.
  • Send the agent questions, not dossiers. Design workflows so the agent works from public documents (ordinances, TUPA, schedules). Personal data enters only where a workflow truly needs it, minimised and logged.
  • Paper the cross-border transfer. Sign the vendor's data-processing agreement, record the transfer in the district's data inventory, and tell residents plainly where the service runs.
  • Keep a human in command. Named officers approve official outputs; every agent action is logged and auditable — the log retention the new regulation expects is at least two years.
  • Protect the exit. Peru's cloud guidance for public entities requires that the district's information remains its exclusive property and does not stay with the provider when the contract ends. Write that into the contract from day one.
06

A collaboration model that fits a small district

The pattern that works — in Peru's own AI regulation and in municipal practice internationally — is: buy the model, build locally only the thin layer that must be Peruvian, and scale in stages with capped budgets. The model is rented by the month for the price of a team lunch; the local layer (WhatsApp front door, connections to rentas and mesa de partes, Spanish content, compliance paperwork) is where a Lima implementation partner earns its fee.

Phase 0 · Weeks 1–4

Get the house in order

Name the AI Officer and confirm the Data Protection Officer. Choose the pilot workflow. Collect and clean the documents the agent will use. Run the xAI availability test.

Phase 1 · Days 30–90

One pilot, capped budget

Launch the resident-attention agent on WhatsApp and the website, inside the regulatory sandbox logic: prepaid balance, hard spending cap, staff reviewing answers daily. Measure questions answered and hours saved.

Phase 2 · Months 4–9

Extend to the back office

Add mesa de partes routing and staff document search. Connect payment-status and appointment systems. Register the systems in the Catálogo IA Perú and publish the district's AI Action Plan.

Phase 3 · Year 2

Scale what proved itself

Add the phone voice agent, more service areas, and the mayor's reporting digest. Renegotiate volumes. Share the playbook with neighbouring districts.

The district provides

  • The AI Officer (OIA) and Data Protection Officer
  • The documents and procedures — the agent is only as good as its sources
  • Officers who approve official outputs
  • The political decision, once per phase, to continue

The implementation partner provides

  • The WhatsApp / web / phone front doors in Spanish
  • Connections to district systems (rentas, mesa de partes, appointments)
  • Answer-quality monitoring and staff training
  • The compliance artefacts: data inventory, transfer contracts, catalogue registration

The model provider provides

  • The AI model and document store, billed by usage (section 4)
  • Contractual privacy terms: no training on district data, deletion, zero-retention options
  • Spending caps and usage reporting on the account
  • No lock-in on the district's documents — they remain district property
Why not build everything in-house? Training or hosting a frontier model is far beyond a district budget — and unnecessary. At district volumes the rented model costs tens of dollars a month. What the district should own is the layer residents actually touch and the data itself; that is also the layer a future administration can move to another provider.
07

Recommendation

Start with Grok, on a 90-day capped pilot, with the homework done first

Recommended path: build the pilot on xAI's Grok with Collections — one account holds the model, the district's documents and the voice option, its prices are published and flat where it matters, and the document-bot route is already proven in Lima's private sector. Name the AI Officer and Data Protection Officer in week one, sign the data-processing terms, and cap the pilot's spend in the account itself.

Two honest caveats. First, xAI does not publish which countries it serves — the pilot starts with a one-day live test of account and billing from Peru, and if that fails, OpenAI is the ready fallback: Peru is officially supported, its document search costs the same, and its budget model is cheaper per answer. Anthropic's Claude is the third option, strongest for long-document drafting, also officially available in Peru. Second, the headline government deals from all three vendors — $1 or $0.42 per agency — are for U.S. agencies only; the district should budget at the commercial rates in section 4, which at this scale are small.

First three moves: (1) appoint the OIA and confirm the ODP; (2) pick the pilot workflow — resident attention on WhatsApp is the strongest candidate; (3) run the xAI availability test and assemble the document set. Everything else follows from those.
